Lead 1, "it will happen by <date>" questions: in the past, these resolved Yes more often than their price said, by — on average. Strongest in geopolitics. Could be a real bias, or an artifact of which markets we sampled (below).

Lead 2, overreaction: after a side jumped 5–15¢ in a day, it was then overpriced by — on average; after a 5–15¢ drop, underpriced by about as much. That is small, but larger than the cost of trading a mid-priced market (about — of the price to buy $1,000).

Dead, at least for now: buying near-certain favorites (90–99¢) and holding to the end. Gains of a cent or two per bet are wiped out by the occasional shock loss of the whole stake. Also no edge in sports, the biggest category: prices there were accurate.

The caveat: every market here traded at least —. Markets where something dramatic happens attract huge trading, so they are over-represented in a "biggest markets" sample, which flatters exactly the bets that won. 1. How accurate are Polymarket prices?

Quite accurate overall. If prices were perfect, every dot below would sit on the diagonal: things priced at 30¢ would happen 30% of the time. The dots stay close to it, with two exceptions: cheap outcomes (5–20¢) happened more often than priced, and favorites (80–95¢) less often. Those are two sides of the same coin.

3. "Yes, it will happen" was underpriced, especially for "by <date>" questions

Another view of the same pattern: across Yes/No markets, how far "Yes" was underpriced 7 days before close. Questions like "Will X happen by March 31?" stand out, as does geopolitics. Common wisdom on Polymarket is the opposite ("bet No, things rarely happen"), which is why I suspect our sample: when the thing does happen, trading explodes and the market lands in our top-volume list. For example, "Khamenei out as Supreme Leader by Feb 28?" traded at a few cents a week before, then resolved Yes and became one of the most-traded markets of the year.

4. Big daily moves partly reverse

For every market and every day in its last month, I compared the price move over the previous 24 hours with how the market later resolved. Sides that just rose 5–15¢ ended up slightly overpriced, and sides that just fell were slightly underpriced: a mild overreaction. Very large moves (>15¢) did not reverse, which suggests those were real news.

7. Multi-outcome markets: do the prices add up?

In a market like "Who will win the election?", exactly one outcome pays $1, so buying every "Yes" should cost about $1. If it costs less, that's risk-free profit. In today's snapshot, — of — events summed to under $1, but only by a few cents, and most likely because cheap outcomes outside our snapshot are missing. Not a finding yet: it needs complete outcome lists and live depth, which is cheap to add to the recorder.
